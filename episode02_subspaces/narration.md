# Seeing Linear Algebra — Episode 2: Span and Vector Subspaces

Approximate target length: 6–8 minutes after narration pacing and pauses.

## One vector
Begin with one nonzero vector. Multiplying it by every real number stretches it forward, shrinks it toward the origin, and reverses it. Together, these multiples fill an entire line through the origin. This is the span of one vector.

## Why the origin matters
A parallel line shifted away from the origin may look almost identical, but it cannot be a subspace. Multiplication by zero must produce the zero vector, so every subspace must contain the origin.

## Two vectors
Now add a second vector pointing in an independent direction. Every choice of two scalars produces a linear combination. As the coefficients vary, these combinations fill the entire plane.

## Dependence
But when the second vector becomes a multiple of the first, the plane collapses. The second vector contributes no new direction, so the dimension returns to one.

## Three dimensions
In three-dimensional space, one independent vector spans a line. Two span a plane. A third independent direction fills all of three-dimensional space. Dimension counts independent directions.

## The test
A subset is a vector subspace when it contains zero, is closed under addition, and is closed under scalar multiplication. Lines and planes through the origin pass. Circles and shifted planes fail.

## Closing
A subspace is not defined merely by its appearance. It is defined by closure: every linear combination of its vectors remains inside it.
